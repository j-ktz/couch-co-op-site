How the 183-day rule counts a day

Under many residency rules, any part of a day in a place counts as a full day there. New York's tax department says it plainly: "Any part of a day is a day for this purpose." So a morning flight out or a lunch stop can each add a day. There are exceptions, so check the rule that applies to you with a tax professional.

Two different "183-day rules"

People say "the 183-day rule" for two separate tests.

TestWho it's aboutThe day count
State residency (New York, for example)People domiciled elsewhere who keep a home in the stateNew York treats you as a resident if you keep a permanent place of abode there for substantially all of the year and spend 184 days or more in the state.
Federal substantial presence test (IRS)People who aren't US citizens or green card holdersAt least 31 days this year, and 183 days over three years counting all days this year, a third of last year's days and a sixth of the year before.

Other states have their own versions. Many use a similar "more than 183 days plus a home" test, but the details differ.

How a day is counted

The key word is presence, not nights. New York's tax department puts it in one line: "Any part of a day is a day for this purpose." The IRS says you are treated as present on any day you are physically present in the country.

What happened (example)How it usually counts
You drove into New York for lunch and left at 3 PMA New York day, under New York's rule
You flew out of New York at 7 AMA New York day
You were in New Jersey and New York on the same dateEach state applies its own rule, so one date can count in both
A layover under 24 hours, between two places outside the USNot counted for the IRS test (an exception)

There are other exceptions (for example, some commuters from Canada or Mexico, and certain visa holders for the IRS test), so check the exact rule that applies to you.

Why a day-by-day record matters

If a tax department asks where you were, you're the one who has to show it, and memory doesn't hold up well a year later. Practitioners commonly point to records like receipts, calendars and phone location history. A record kept as you go, with where each entry came from, is easier to stand behind than one rebuilt from a credit card statement.

Quick answers

Does part of a day count as a day?

Under many state rules, yes. New York's tax department says any part of a day is a day for its residency count. The IRS treats you as present on any day you are physically in the US, with some exceptions.

Is the 183-day rule the same in every state?

No. Each state sets its own test, and the federal substantial presence test is a separate rule with its own weighted formula.

Does Statecount tell me if I'm a resident?

No. Statecount keeps records. It is not tax or legal advice. A tax professional can tell you how the rules apply to you.
